#114: Prayer, War, and the Next Generation: A Conversation with Dennis Melnichuk

August 4, 2026

Standing in the kitchen with his wife, just days before Russia’s full-scale invasion of Ukraine, Dennis Melnichuk felt a single word settle on both their hearts: come, you need to go out west. They gathered a team, told everyone to bring their passports, and left on what looked like an ordinary missions trip. Three days later the war began, and the roads heading east, toward the people trying to escape, were nearly empty. In this episode of Missions Pulse, Dennis, founder of Awakened Generation and a missionary who moved from China to Ukraine in 2019, joins us from Chiang Mai to talk about what it means to stay when everything says go, and why prayer, not activity, has been the thing that kept him in the fight.

Dennis’s path to Ukraine began with a leap he describes as radical: in June 2019, with little context and no clear plan, he packed up his life in China and went where he felt the Lord leading. He started traveling the country with teams and local partners, visiting churches along the old Donbass front lines, where an evangelical “mega church” once meant about fifty people. When the invasion came, that team helped organize evacuations and pulled thousands of people out of dangerous areas. Today Awakened Generation runs a missions base, a coffee shop, and a prayer room, and hosts conferences and events built for young people. The vision behind it all is simple: before anyone learns to do the works of Christ, they need to know they are a son or daughter first.

The conversation doesn’t stay in the comfortable places for long. Dennis describes the leadership vacuum the war created, with pastors fleeing and men drafted, and the young people who have stepped into it, praying for the sick and preaching in the streets. He admits that during the hardest stretch of the war he burned out so completely that he couldn’t pray at all, and for about three weeks he set an alarm for 5:15 a.m., lay on the floor, and said only “Help me.” He tells of a trip into the Amazon where a group of soldiers, then a line of villagers, came asking for prayer. Host David shares a story of his own, how adopting his daughter in Thailand opened his eyes to what spiritual adoption means. Running through all of it is the idea that mission fueled by urgency alone eventually hardens the heart, and that the prayer room is what keeps a team from becoming a machine.

The episode also asks how this model can travel. Dennis and David compare notes on pairing a coffee shop with a prayer room, a place where non-believers can wander in for an open mic night or a “Bible study for non-religious” and meet people who have just come from the presence of God. Dennis points to the Moravians, whose hundred-year prayer vigil launched one of the largest missions movements in Protestant history, as proof that prayer and action belong together. He names the biggest threat to revival in one word, “ego,” and the biggest obstacle to missions as an unwillingness to relinquish what is comfortable. His own picture of success is a modest one: sons and daughters who become fathers and mothers, and a ministry that eventually doesn’t need him.

This episode is a reminder that the mission is not a project to be managed but a life to be given, and that the road to sacrifice, as Dennis puts it, “leads also to resurrection.”
